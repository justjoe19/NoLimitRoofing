A roof is one of the larger investments most homeowners make in their property, and the contractor doing the work matters as much as the material going on the roof. These are the questions worth asking before you sign anything — and a contractor's willingness to answer them clearly is itself useful information.

## Are you licensed, bonded and insured?

This should be a direct yes with documentation available, not a vague reassurance. Ask to see it if you're not sure how to verify it independently.

## What manufacturer certifications do you hold?

Factory certification (GAF, IKO, Owens Corning, Malarkey, Atlas, CertainTeed, SRS, and others) means a manufacturer has verified the contractor's installation methods, and it's typically required to access the manufacturer's stronger warranty tiers. A contractor with no certifications isn't necessarily bad, but it's a fair question to ask why.

## Who actually supervises the crew on my roof?

Some contractors sell the job and subcontract the actual installation to a crew they don't directly oversee. Ask specifically who's supervising the work and how hands-on that supervision is.

## What happens if you find bad decking?

A contractor who has a clear, upfront answer to this (how it's priced, whether you'll be shown the damage) is more trustworthy than one who hasn't thought about it or gives a vague "we'll handle it."

## Can I get the estimate in writing, with materials specified by name?

A verbal quote or a one-line total isn't a real estimate. See our related article on [what a roofing estimate should include](/learning-center/what-should-be-included-in-a-roofing-estimate.html) for the full list of what to expect in writing.

## What's your warranty, and what voids it?

Ask about both the manufacturer's material warranty and the contractor's own workmanship warranty, and what actions (like DIY repairs by someone else) could void either one.

## Can I see examples of your actual work?

Photos of completed jobs, ideally with some context (location, scope, material used) rather than generic stock imagery, tell you more than a polished sales pitch.

## How do you handle a customer who isn't happy with something?

This is as much about how the answer is given as what it says — a contractor confident in their process will have a real answer, not a defensive one.
