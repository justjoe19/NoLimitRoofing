It's the first question almost every homeowner asks when something goes wrong with their roof: do I need a repair, or a whole new roof? The honest answer is "it depends" — but it depends on a fairly small number of factors, and once you know what they are, the decision usually isn't that hard to make.

## Start with the age of the roof

Every roofing material has a rough expected service life — asphalt shingles commonly run somewhere in the 20-30 year range depending on the specific product and how well it was installed and ventilated. A roof that's 5 years old with a localized problem is almost always a repair. A roof that's 22 years old with the same problem is a different conversation, because you're likely to be back with another repair in a year or two regardless.

## How widespread is the damage?

A single damaged area — a section of missing shingles from wind, a leak traced to one piece of failed flashing — is usually a repair candidate, provided the rest of the roof and the decking underneath are sound. Damage spread across multiple areas of the roof, or multiple separate leaks showing up in different parts of the house, points more toward replacement, since it suggests the roofing system as a whole is wearing out rather than one component failing.

## What does the decking look like underneath?

This is the one that's hard to know without an actual inspection. Soft, rotted, or damaged decking changes the calculation — it needs to be replaced regardless, and once a roof is opened up that far, it often makes more sense to complete a full replacement than to patch a repair on top of new decking in one spot while the rest of the old roof stays in place.

## How many times has this roof already been repaired?

If you're looking at your third or fourth repair call in a few years, the math usually favors replacement even if any single repair is inexpensive — the cumulative cost, plus the ongoing risk of a leak between service calls, tends to outweigh the upfront cost difference over time.

## Energy and comfort, not just leaks

An aging roof doesn't just risk leaking — worn ventilation and insulation tied to an old roofing system can also mean a harder-working HVAC system and less consistent indoor temperatures. That's not a reason by itself to replace a roof that's otherwise sound, but it's a real factor worth weighing if you're already on the fence, since a new roofing system installed with correct ventilation can make a measurable difference in how the house feels, not just how the roof performs in a storm.

## Weighing the cost of "wait and see"

Delaying an obviously-needed replacement to spread out the cost is a real, reasonable option in some situations — but it comes with a tradeoff. A roof that's actively failing can allow water intrusion that damages decking, insulation, or interior finishes, turning a roofing project into a roofing-plus-repair project. If you're choosing to wait, it's worth having an honest conversation about how much runway the roof realistically has left, so "wait and see" is a planned decision rather than a guess.

## What a second opinion is actually worth

If a contractor tells you that you need a full replacement and something about that doesn't sit right, getting a second, independent inspection is a reasonable step — a second look either confirms the recommendation or gives you a different, equally specific assessment to compare it against. We'd rather you feel confident in the decision than take our word for it without question.

## When in doubt, get an actual inspection

None of these factors are something you can reliably judge from the ground or from photos. A roof that looks fine from the driveway can have real problems, and a roof that looks alarming from below can sometimes be a straightforward repair.
